\subsection{\label{sec:level2}Pipeline of 3D Anisotropic Mesh Generation}
\begin{figure}
\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figs/pipline.pdf}
\caption{\label{fig:pip}
automatically numbered.}
\end{figure}
Our paper proposes an automated framework for generating 3D anisotropic, semi-structured boundary layer meshes. Diverging from the conventional paradigm of global Partial Differential Equation (PDE) solvers , this framework adopts a neural field generation approach driven by boundary information. This section outlines the complete, highly automated workflow for generating 3D hybrid anisotropic meshes for specific geometries.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{3D Surface Parsing and Spatial Discretization:}Read a 3D volume mesh file containing a single boundary layer, and utilize spatial volume mesh generation alongside curvature-weight-driven surface sampling to generate the set of physical spatial sampling points for subsequent neural network training.
    \item \textbf{Construction of the Global Geometric Guidance Field (GGGF):}By leveraging KD-Tree acceleration coupled with local barycentric coordinate interpolation, a robust Global Geometric Guidance Field is constructed within the computational domain. This field provides high-quality geometric priors to guide the neural network training.
    \item \textbf{Training of the Multiresolution Boundary-Constrained Neural Field (MBCNF):}We construct and train a boundary-informed neural network based on a multiresolution hash encoding method, termed the Multiresolution Boundary-Constrained Neural Field. A composite loss function—integrating boundary cosine similarity and a Laplacian smoothing physical loss—is employed. This formulation guides the network to learn a continuous and smooth 3D correction vector field, ultimately enabling the prediction of the boundary layer growth direction for target points across the entire global domain.
    \item \textbf{High-Quality 3D Advancing Front Generation:}The MBCNF is utilized to predict the corrective growth directions for the boundary layer. By dynamically adjusting the distance-based weighting between the predicted correction and the rigid boundary constraints, the method effectively steers the layer-by-layer extrusion of the prismatic boundary layer mesh. Coupled with a rigorous tetrahedral quality inspection equipped with a specified tolerance, this step guarantees high mesh quality even in geometrically complex convex and concave regions. Ultimately, this yields a 3D anisotropic prismatic boundary layer mesh suitable for engineering computations.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{\label{sec:level2}Curvature-Driven Spatial Sampling Strategy}
To empower the neural network to effectively capture sharp and intricate geometric features, and to synergize with the subsequent Laplacian smoothing loss function, a comprehensive non-uniform spatial sampling mechanism is established:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Volumetric Discretization of the Computational Domain:}Utilizing the tetrahedral tessellation techniques provided by the TetGen library, a discrete point sequence is generated within the annular domain ($\Omega$) bounded by the inner and outer boundaries. Concurrently, a hierarchical point distribution is enforced along the wall-normal direction to adequately resolve the gradient variations transitioning from the inner to the outer boundary.
    \item \textbf{Geometric Singularity Identification:}Regions exhibiting drastic geometric variations are identified by evaluating the discrete curvature. For the target sampling point sets located on the inner boundary and within the annular physical domain $\Omega$, this curvature is assessed utilizing the normal vector set $\mathbf{N}_{adj}$ from their $k$ adjacent faces (for spatial points within $\Omega$, the adjacent faces are determined via mapping to the nearest inner boundary).
    \item \textbf{Importance Weighting Function:}As shown in the formula\ref{eq:sampling_weight}, a sampling weight $W_i$ is defined to quantitatively represent these geometric singularities. During the subsequent sampling phase for the point sets on the inner boundary and within the domain $\Omega$, a probabilistic sampling strategy is executed based on these computed weights.
    \begin{equation}
        W_i = 1.0 - \min_{n \in N_{\text{adj}}} (\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{n}_{\text{ref}}) + \epsilon
    \label{eq:sampling_weight}
    \end{equation}
    And a regularization constant is incorporated into the formulation to strictly prevent zero weights, thereby ensuring comprehensive sampling coverage across the entire global domain.
    \item \textbf{Uniform Sampling for the Outer Boundary:}Conversely, a uniform sampling approach is adopted for the outer boundary to maintain baseline geometric constraints.
\end{itemize}
\subsection{\label{sec:level2}Global Geometric Guidance Field}
To alleviate the training burden on the neural network, we construct a Multiresolution Boundary-Constrained Neural Field (MBCNF), denoted as $\mathbf{u}_{mbcnf}$, which is intrinsically defined by the normal vectors of the inner boundary ($\mathbf{n}_{inner}$). This field dynamically modulates the strength of spatial constraints via a distance function $f(d)$. Specifically, it imposes a strong orthogonal prior in the near-wall region to guarantee the quality of the generated boundary layer. Conversely, in the far-field region, it serves as a baseline field that couples with the residual field predicted by the neural network to asymptotically approach the target direction of the outer boundary. This formulation bypasses the necessity for the model to approximate complex geometric manifold features from scratch. Instead, it casts the prediction objective into a residual learning paradigm, wherein the neural network is solely tasked with learning and predicting the local residuals between the geometric prior field and the target field.

The construction procedure for $\mathbf{u}_{mbcnf}$ is detailed as follows: For an arbitrary spatial point $\mathbf{p}$ located on or outside the inner boundary, a KD-Tree spatial index is employed to retrieve its $K$ nearest triangular faces on the inner boundary. We compute the exact projection of point $\mathbf{p}$ onto each respective face along with its corresponding barycentric coordinates. Subsequently, incorporating the Euclidean distances between point $\mathbf{p}$ and these $K$ adjacent faces, an Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) scheme is applied to seamlessly fuse the discrete normal information from these faces. This procedure establishes a globally continuous initial reference field. Finally, by superimposing the corrective vector predicted by the neural network onto this initial field, the definitive growth normal vector is obtained (as formulated in Section 3.4).
\subsection{\label{sec:level2}Multiresolution Boundary-Constrained Neural Field}
Traditional coordinate-based networks relying on Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) are fundamentally bottlenecked by the inherent "spectral bias," rendering them inadequate for accurately resolving potential high-frequency, complex geometric features within the boundary layer regions of intricate 3D models. To circumvent this limitation, our paper introduces a multiresolution hash encoding architecture. By leveraging learnable, hierarchical hash tables, this architecture decouples spatial geometric features into multi-scale local basis functions, thereby significantly enhancing the model's representational capacity for localized geometric details.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Suppression of Numerical Oscillations via $C^2$ Continuous Interpolation: }Conventional hash encoding schemes typically employ linear interpolation, which intrinsically causes the output field to forfeit first- and second-order derivative continuity across hash grid boundaries. During the evaluation of Laplacian smoothing constraints, such discontinuities readily precipitate singularities in the physical loss formulation. This subsequently induces non-physical numerical oscillations in the generated vector field that unacceptably align with the underlying hash grid. To address this critical issue, we introduce a $C^2$ continuous, higher-order smooth interpolation function. From a foundational mathematical perspective, this function strictly guarantees the global $C^2$ continuity of the predicted vector field, $\mathbf{u}_{pred}(\mathbf{p})$. Consequently, it perfectly accommodates the computational requirements of second-order partial differential operators and effectively suppresses non-physical abrupt mutations along the spatial extrusion directions.
    \item \textbf{Hard Constraint Formulation via Boundary-Informed Neural Networks (BINN):}A boundary-augmented formulation is employed to re-parameterize the predictive output of the neural network:
    \begin{equation}
        u_{pred}(p) = \gamma \cdot u_{bce}(p) + f(d) \cdot \Phi(p; \theta)
        \label{eq:u_pred}
    \end{equation}
    where $\mathbf{u}_{mbcnf}(\mathbf{p})$ denotes the global geometric guidance field(GGGF) constructed via surface manifold projection, and $\Phi(\mathbf{p}; \theta)$ represents the corrective vector field predicted by the multiresolution hash network. The core distance modulation function, $f(d)$, establishes a distance-driven dynamic confidence mechanism. As a spatial point approaches the geometric boundary ($d \to 0$), the weighting of the residual term $\Phi$ smoothly decays to absolute zero. This elegantly ensures that the predicted field $\mathbf{u}_{pred}(\mathbf{p})$ automatically and rigorously degenerates into the exact Dirichlet boundary condition. This mathematical construction not only inherently enforces a "hard constraint" on the orthogonality of the near-wall mesh but also profoundly simplifies the neural network's optimization objective. By shifting the paradigm from approximating a complex, full-scale geometric field to merely learning low-frequency localized deviations, it significantly accelerates the convergence process.
\end{itemize}
\subsection{\label{sec:level2}Loss Functions}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Boundary Cosine Loss:}o quantify the directional discrepancy between the predicted vector field and the target normal vectors, a weighted cosine similarity metric is employed as the geometric boundary loss:
    \begin{equation}
        L_{BC} = \int_{\partial\Omega} \left( 1 - \frac{\mathbf{u}_{pred} \cdot \mathbf{n}_{target}}{\|\mathbf{u}_{pred}\| \|\mathbf{n}_{target}\|} \right) d\Gamma
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Laplacian Smoothing Loss:}To prevent the intersection of internal field lines and the formation of singularities, thereby ensuring a seamless geometric transition of the boundary layer mesh from the inner to the outer boundary, a physical regularization term based on the finite difference operator, $\nabla^2 \mathbf{u}$, is introduced. This term rigorously enforces a spatial smoothness constraint across the vector field and is efficiently executed via large-scale parallel finite difference computations on the GPU.
\end{itemize}
\subsection{\label{sec:level2}Advancing Front Mesh Generation}
As shown in the formula \ref{eq:new_point}, we employ the Advancing Front Method (AFM) to guide the boundary layer nodes in generating volumetric mesh elements after acquiring the converged predictive corrective vector field, $\mathbf{u}_{pred}$.The new point for the subsequent layer is computed by advancing along the normalized vector field direction from each corresponding point on the current front.
\begin{equation}
    p_j^{(i+1)} = p_j^{(i)} + h_i \cdot \frac{u_{\text{pred}}(p_j^{(i)})}{\|u_{\text{pred}}(p_j^{(i)})\|}
    \label{eq:new_point}
\end{equation}
The step length (layer thickness) $h_i$
is determined by the initial thickness $h_0$ and a growth rate $r: h_i = h_0 · r^i$.

To circumvent severe geometric defects—such as mesh self-intersections, elemental topological inversions, and saddle-face distortions—that frequently manifest during the advancement over complex 3D surfaces, we introduce a rigorous Two-Stage Manifold Quality Validation mechanism into the mesh topological growth pipeline:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Macroscopic Topological Check:}During the initial phase of element generation, the algorithm extracts the base and top faces of the nascent prismatic element to compute their respective unit normal vectors, $\mathbf{n}_{base}$ and $\mathbf{n}_{top}$. By evaluating the dot product of these vectors, a geometric constraint is imposed on the macroscopic growth posture of the mesh. If $\mathbf{n}_{base} \cdot \mathbf{n}_{top} < -\epsilon$, the system identifies a severe "fold-over" or global topological inversion and directly intercepts the progression. This procedure fundamentally prevents non-physical folding of the growth direction at a macroscopic level.
    \item \textbf{Microscopic Jacobian Tolerance Validation:}For prismatic elements that successfully pass the macroscopic check, the algorithm further executes a microscopic volume validation predicated on tetrahedral decomposition. The prism is identically decomposed into three non-overlapping tetrahedral subsets. The signed volume of each constituent tetrahedron, which is strictly proportional to the local Jacobian determinant, is computed via the scalar triple product of the spatial vectors: $\mathbf{v}_3 \cdot (\mathbf{v}_1 \times \mathbf{v}_2)$. The volume sign is rigorously preserved during computation to accurately delineate the topological orientation of the element within 3D space. If the accumulated total volume, $V_{total}$, yields a negative value, it signifies an overall elemental inversion.
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Tolerance Mechanism for Local Distortion:}Recognizing that slight geometric distortions (e.g., the formation of saddle faces) on the lateral faces are inevitable when advancing through boundary regions characterized by high curvature or drastic convex-concave alternations, this study eschews the stringent "one-size-fits-all" criterion that demands all sub-tetrahedral volumes be strictly positive. Instead, a relative tolerance mechanism parametrically tied to the total volume (empirically set to $5\%$ in this paper) is introduced. The algorithm permits constituent tetrahedra to exhibit marginal negative volumes (i.e., $V_i \ge -|V_{total}| \times 5\%$). The mesh is deemed invalid only when the negative volume induced by local distortion exceeds this specified threshold. While mathematically ensuring the preservation of the mesh manifold structure, this mechanism significantly bolsters the survivability and advancing robustness of the AFM algorithm in extremely complex geometric domains.
\end{itemize}

All models were implemented in PyTorch and trained on an NVIDIA RTX 3050Ti GPU (with CPU fallback capability). The proposed neural network architecture consists of two tightly coupled components: a multi-resolution HashGrid encoder and a Tiny MLP decoder. Specifically, the HashGrid takes 3D spatial coordinates ($x, y, z$) as input and maps them into a 32-dimensional concatenated feature vector utilizing 16 hash levels (with 2 feature dimensions per level). This encoded multiresolution feature vector is subsequently fed into the input layer of the Tiny MLP. The Tiny MLP comprises two hidden layers with 64 neurons each and utilizes the SiLU (Swish) activation function to predict the final 3D normal correction vector.The core hyperparameters, including loss weights and mesh generation settings, are summarized in Table \ref{tab:hyperparameters}.

Key parameters for the framework were set as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Geometric Features:} The K-Nearest Neighbors algorithm used $K = 20$ neighbors to robustly interpolate the target surface normals and calculate the weighted average distance.
    
    \item \textbf{Loss Function:} The composite loss function prioritized geometric accuracy and network stability, with the inner boundary loss assigned a weight of $1.0$ and the outer boundary loss a weight of $0.001$ to ensure strict orthogonality in critical near-body regions. Additionally, a PDE loss weight of $1 \times 10^{-3}$ was incorporated to mitigate local distortions.
    
    \item \textbf{Mesh Generation:} The advancing-front algorithm was configured to generate $30$ layers with a highly refined initial thickness of $5 \times 10^{-5}$ and a layer-to-layer growth rate of $1.2$.
\end{itemize}

To evaluate the critical role of the boundary cosine loss function, we conducted a comparative ablation study using a 3D airplane geometry model, assessing the mesh generation quality optimized by three different loss functions. As illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:angleloss}, the prismatic boundary layer mesh generated by the model utilizing the boundary cosine loss significantly outperforms those optimized by the other two baseline methods.

Specifically, Figure \ref{fig:angleloss1} displays the result optimized using the $L_2$ norm loss, defined as $\mathcal{L}_{norm\_L2} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} (\|\mathbf{v}_i\| - 1)^2$. Although this formulation enforces a unit magnitude for the vectors, it completely lacks directional guidance. Consequently, the neural network fails to accurately capture the correct surface orientation, leading to a substantial number of unnatural depressions on the generated mesh surface.

Figure \ref{fig:angleloss3} shows the result optimized with the squared chord length distance loss, formulated as $\mathcal{L}_{chord\_sq} = \frac{1}{N_{bc}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{bc}} \|\mathbf{v}_i - \mathbf{n}_i\|_2^2$. While this Euclidean distance-based loss implicitly encourages alignment in both magnitude and direction, it lacks sufficient sensitivity to pure angular deviations, resulting in the local accumulation of directional errors. Thus, although it yields an improvement over Figure (a), it still fails to completely eliminate the anomalous surface depressions.

In contrast, our proposed boundary cosine loss method, shown in Figure \ref{fig:angleloss2}, is defined as: $\mathcal{L}_{bc\_outer} = \frac{1}{N_{bc}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{bc}} (1 - \cos(\mathbf{\hat{u}}_i, \mathbf{n}_i))$. By utilizing cosine similarity to isolate and explicitly penalize angular inconsistencies, this formulation forces the generated surface normals to strictly align with the target boundary. This rigorous directional constraint effectively eradicates all anomalous depressions, yielding a high-quality prismatic surface mesh.

Furthermore, we conducted identical comparative experiments on four other distinct geometric models and performed a quantitative quality assessment of the generated prismatic boundary layer volume meshes. As reported in Table \ref{tab:ablation_study：angleloss}, the meshes optimized with the boundary cosine loss consistently and significantly outperform those generated by the other two methods across all evaluated quality metrics.

This phenomenon perfectly aligns with our theoretical expectations. Consequently, the directional guidance for mesh growth relies increasingly on the network's generalized output field within the continuous domain.

Without the explicit spatial smoothness prior provided by the PDE loss, the highly sensitive multi-resolution HashGrid tends to produce localized high-frequency oscillations in these less-constrained deep regions. During the layer-by-layer numerical integration of the advancing-front algorithm, these microscopic directional errors accumulate and are amplified, ultimately leading to significant degradation in deep-layer mesh quality and the emergence of surface depressions. Conversely, the PDE loss enforces rigorous second-order continuity across the entire computational domain. This effectively compensates for the decay of the boundary supervision signal in deeper regions, fundamentally suppresses the generation of microscopic ripples, and ensures the robust and high-quality growth of meshes.
